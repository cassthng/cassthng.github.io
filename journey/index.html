Who I am

Three ways of seeing

I came to SUTD because I wanted to make things, not just study them. In my first year I helped build a launcher that won its cohort challenge, a car that climbed steps with a rubber band, and a wearable that speaks for people who can’t easily be heard. Each one started as a sketch and only became real after it failed, got rebuilt and was tested again.

Outside the lab I live two other lives. I teach, at a tuition centre, with private students and as an essay marker, which has taught me that understanding something means being able to explain it simply. And I photograph: weddings, church events, the streets of every city I visit, very often on film.

These three habits feed each other. Engineering made me patient with iteration. Teaching made me clear. Photography made me notice people. Together they’re how I plan to meet a new country, a new campus and a new set of classmates.

The most robust solution isn’t adding more moving parts. It’s having the clarity to strip away what fails.

The engineer

CAD in Rhino and Onshape, 3D printing and Python, plus React and Firebase at work. I simplify first, then test, then refine.

The teacher

Featured project · 03.007B Innovating with Design and AI · 2026

SpeakEasy: built to be heard

Imagine ordering your favourite meal at a packed hawker centre and not being understood. That’s an everyday reality for people with speech impairments, and it’s the problem my team set out to solve.

SpeakEasy is a compact wearable, clipped on as a badge or worn as a necklace, that captures speech, cuts through background noise and plays a clear, synthesised voice in real time. We designed it for dignity as much as function: something discreet that people would actually choose to wear.

Our user research found that 75% of hawkers struggle to hear speech-impaired customers during peak hours. That number kept us honest. We dropped bulkier early ideas, a motorised anti-muffle mask and AI eye-tracking glasses, and focused on one form that feels natural.

The hard part was the trade-off between speed and accuracy. Vosk ran fast on-device but made more mistakes; Whisper was slower but far more reliable in noisy settings. We tuned the pipeline around real-world chaos, then moved from a tangle of breadboards to a fully integrated CAD housing.

80%accuracy in speech transcription and synthesis on the working prototype

Discover

Interviewed hawkers and mapped the moments communication breaks down.

Explore

Sketched masks, glasses and wearables; picked the one people would wear.

Build

Raspberry Pi, microphones and speakers, then a custom CAD enclosure.

Test

Benchmarked Vosk against Whisper in noise and tuned for accuracy.

What it taught me

Good engineering starts with people. The best technical choice was the one that made the device something a person would feel proud, not awkward, to wear. Built with Ho Shu Rui, Amoz Low, Ethan Loh and Ryan Ng.

01.018 Design Thinking Project I · Physical World

Cannon & Projectile Challenge: champions

One of my very first SUTD projects, and we won it. The brief: build a launcher and predict exactly where its projectile will land. Not just fire it, but call the shot.

  • Modelled the trajectory from first principles: launch angle, velocity and distance.
  • Calibrated through round after round of test fires until predictions matched reality.
  • 3D-printed the barrel and a custom projectile built around a tennis ball.
  • Scored on accuracy: 5 points in the laundry bag, 3 in the pool, 2 in the outer ring.
1stplace in the cohort challenge

10.015 Physical World · 1D Challenge · SC01 Group 5

The car that climbed by letting go

An autonomous vehicle had to carry a 200 g load over a multi-step course. Our first design used a 5:1 gear train on a torsion spring. Its peak torque stripped the printed teeth after two turns.

  • Swapped gears for an axle-wound rubber-band drive: smoother, sustained torque.
  • Fitted oversized, rubber-band-treaded wheels to clear 1–4 cm steps.
  • Designed a low, wide basket to keep the payload centred while climbing.
  • Cut a snagging zip-tie release minutes before the run.
200 gdelivered cleanly to the finish line

10.016 Science for a Sustainable World · Mar 2026

Life cycle analysis of a badminton racket

We traced a racket’s carbon footprint from raw material to landfill in Granta EduPack. I led the goal and scope, the inventory analysis and the impact assessment. The answer was stark: the carbon fibre frame alone made up 93% of the racket’s CO₂, because carbonising the fibres needs extreme heat.

Using property charts and performance indices, we replaced carbon fibre with a flax fibre composite and nylon strings with partially bio-based PA410, keeping the same mass and feel.

10.024 Spatial Design World · Class 3 Group 6 · 2026

Domus Orationis: a house of prayer

A contemplative retreat among the rice terraces of Mù Cang Chải in Vietnam’s highlands. Sunlit timber lattices open the upper floors to the hills, while a hidden adoration room is carved into the slope below, reached by a spiral stair.

We generated the form from three lines, swept, joined, sliced and hollowed, then moved from sketches to plans, an exploded axonometric, interior renders and a landscaped 3D-printed model.

Six years behind the lens · Nikon F2 & Fuji GW690II · @cassidy.films

Thirty-six frames at a time

Why film

Film has no screen to check. You watch the light, feel the rhythm of the room and trust your timing on every frame. When I shot my close friends’ wedding entirely on black-and-white film, that discipline let me focus on what mattered: the quiet gestures, the sudden laughter, the glances in between.

I shoot on a 35mm Nikon F2 and a medium-format Fuji GW690II, and publish the work on @cassidy.films, now more than 130 posts. Commissions include weddings, retreats and church events such as the Catholic Pitch mixer, and a camera comes with me everywhere I travel.

What it means for GEXP

GEXP asks every participant to come home with photos and a video of their exchange. That’s the part I’m most excited about. I’ll return with a body of work that shows SUTD’s exchange life properly, the kind of images that make the next cohort want to apply.
